When AI Translates a Word, Whose Meaning Does It Choose?

Si Wu

October 20, 2026

A small experiment on LLMs, overtranslation, and culturally dense Chinese terms

Choose a wordCompare contextsCompare models

A growing scientific literature examines how large language models may reshape work, productivity, and task performance, from writing to customer support and software development (Acemoglu 2025; Eloundou, Manning, Mishkin, Rock 2024; Noy and Zhang 2023; Peng et al. 2023; Brynjolfsson et al. 2023). Companies like Google, IBM, and Microsoft also use large language models (LLMs) in various ways, including email text suggestions, language classifications, and content recommendations.

A related literature evaluates LLMs as translation systems. Recent studies ask how well Generative Pre-trained Transformer (GPT) models translate across languages, how prompting strategies affect translation quality, and whether document-level or context-aware prompts improve performance (Hendy et al. 2023; Jiao et al. 2023; Vilar et al. 2022; Zhang et al. 2023; Wang et al. 2023). Other work applies LLM translation to scientific research, showing both high accuracy and a more subtle problem: domain experts sometimes prefer not to translate certain technical terms in English when translation makes them less familiar (Kleidermacher and Zou 2026). This blog post builds on that literature but shifts the unit of analysis: instead of asking whether an LLM can translate a sentence or document well, I ask whether it can preserve the contextual meaning of culturally and philosophically dense Chinese terms whose English glosses are useful but incomplete.

Existing work on LLM translation often evaluates accuracy at the sentence, document, or domain level. My experiment focuses on the smaller unit possible: culturally and philosophically dense Chinese terms, which are often made up of only one Chinese character. These Chinese terms are not impossible to translate, but their translations are often partial and don’t capture the full cultural, historical, and philosophical meanings behind the terms. The question that underlines the experiment is therefore not only whether an LLM can translate across languages, but whether it can explain what its own translation leaves out when it happens.

From Overtranslation to Culturally Dense Terms

Kleidermacher and Zou (2026) handle that subjectivity through in-context learning: they give GPT-4o a curated example from a scientific article whose author has reviewed the translation and identified terms that should remain in English. That example then guides how the model translates later texts.

This blog post adapts that idea to a different problem. Instead of asking which scientific terms should remain in English, I ask how LLMs handle culturally and philosophically dense Chinese terms whose English translations are useful but incomplete. A summary of these terms and why they are culturally and philosophically dense is presented in table 1. For example, should 仁 ren simply be translated as "benevolence"? Should 礼 li become "ritual" or "etiquette"? Should 道 dao be translated as "the Way"?

The experiment focuses on two prompt conditions. In the term-only condition, the model sees only the Chinese term and is asked for a common English translation and what may be lost. In the passage-context condition, the model sees the term inside a short sentence. This comparison is inspired by the following question: what changes when the model is given contextual information that guides how a term should be handled?

Terms and Passage Contexts

The experiment uses five classical or philosophical Chinese terms. The common English gloss is not wrong, but it is often too thin to convey the meaning the term carries in context.

Experiment Design

01 / The reader experiment

Choose an English word

Choose a Chinese term and one of the three English translations offered. You will then see a constructed Chinese passage, its English rendering, and a short editorial note about what your chosen word captures or leaves out.

English rendering prepared for this reader exercise; the original Chinese sentence was supplied to the models.

The next experiment compares two prompt conditions. In the term-only condition, the model receives a Chinese term without a surrounding sentence and is asked to provide a common English translation, explain possible meanings, and identify uncertainty. In the passage + interpretive note condition, it receives the term within a short Chinese passage, together with a brief English context note, and is asked to ground its explanation in that passage. Both conditions also request assessments of translation fit and potential meaning loss. We can then compare the responses to examine how the additional context shapes the model’s interpretation.

How to read the response fields

These labels are the responding model’s own assessments. The explanations below are a reader’s guide.

Translation fit
How well the compact English translation captures the Chinese term in the available context. The allowed labels are strong, partial, weak, and misleading. “Partial” suggests that the English wording captures some meaning but needs explanation.
Context needed
How much contextual information the model judges necessary to interpret the term: low, medium, or high. In the passage condition, a high rating may indicate that the supplied passage and note still leave questions unresolved. This field is not a measure of how much context the prompt contains.
Flattening risk
The model’s assessment of the risk that a compact English rendering compresses or obscures cultural, historical, philosophical, or relational meaning: low, medium, or high. High risk does not establish that the model’s explanation actually lost that meaning.
Alternative interpretations
Other meanings or interpretive frames the model considers plausible or potentially overlooked. These are model-generated possibilities, not equally valid readings or independently verified definitions. The prompt asks for “none” only when the model sees no real plausible alternative.

A translation can have partial fit while flattening risk remains high: a useful English gloss can still leave important meaning unresolved. Providers may also apply these labels differently.

What this evidence can tell us

The explanations and ratings presented here were generated by models in a saved exploratory run. The explanations deserve attention because they allow us to examine what an English translation captures, what requires further explanation, and how the model’s reading changes when a passage and an interpretive note are supplied. This small experiment offers a starting point for studying LLM translation at the level of individual Chinese characters that are philosophical terms. The next step is to examine whether the models’ explanations reflect the meanings expressed in the Chinese passages and whether different readers would interpret the same passages differently.
